Contax RTS III: The Last Word in Manual-Focus Flagships

Contax RTS III: The Last Word in Manual-Focus Flagships

If the Contax RTS started the German–Japanese legend, the RTS III was its high point. It appeared in 1990, when autofocus cameras had already taken over the market. Contax, by then part of Kyocera, chose to launch a flagship SLR that stayed with manual focus, and it refined every part of the traditional professional body as far as it could go.

Going against the tide

From the mid-1980s onwards, the major manufacturers moved to autofocus, including for their professional flagships. Contax took a very different path. The Zeiss C/Y lenses were manual-focus designs, and the brand's core value lay in optical quality rather than focusing speed. So the RTS III concentrated on image quality, metering accuracy and reliability. It was aimed at professionals and serious enthusiasts who wanted the best possible image quality.

The ceramic vacuum pressure plate: starting with the film plane

The RTS III's best-known innovation is its ceramic vacuum pressure plate, which Contax called the RTV (Real Time Vacuum) system.

Film is never perfectly flat, and it curls slightly as temperature and humidity change. With ordinary lenses this hardly matters. With high-resolution Zeiss lenses, especially when shooting wide open, even a small departure from the film plane can shift the point of focus. The RTS III's ceramic pressure plate has tiny holes in it. When you take a picture, suction holds the film against its flat ceramic surface, so the whole frame stays in one plane and the Zeiss lenses can resolve all the detail they are capable of.

Improving image quality by starting with the film plane was very unusual in a 35mm SLR, and nothing shows Contax's devotion to image quality more clearly.

Professional specifications

The RTS III made no compromises on specification:

  • Fast shutter: an electronically controlled focal-plane shutter with a top speed of 1/8000 sec and flash sync at 1/250 sec, which was top-tier at the time.
  • Full-coverage viewfinder: close to 100% coverage, so what you see is what you get, which helps with precise framing.
  • Multiple metering modes: including centre-weighted and spot, with exposure compensation and auto bracketing for precise control of exposure.
  • Built-in motor: the film winder is built in, so you can shoot continuously without buying an accessory.
  • Mirror lock-up: this reduces vibration at slow shutter speeds, which matters most in macro and landscape work.

Carrying on the "real time" idea

The RTS III kept the core idea of the RTS series: an electromagnetic shutter release that fires at the lightest touch. The delay between pressing the button and the actual exposure is extremely short, so photographers can capture the moment precisely. This feel ran from the first RTS through to the RTS III and became the most recognisable trait of the whole series.

The body keeps the clean style of its predecessors, with clear and easy-to-use dials. It is sturdier overall, though, and its weight and size reflect its role as a professional tool.

A perfect match for Zeiss lenses

The RTS III uses the C/Y mount and accepts the full range of Zeiss T* lenses. From ultra-wide Distagons and standard Planars to telephoto Sonnars, plus several macro and special-purpose lenses, they form a complete professional system. The vacuum pressure plate lets these lenses reach their full optical potential. That partnership between body and lens is the RTS III's greatest strength.

The end of an era

The RTS III stayed in production for more than a decade as the flagship of Contax's manual-focus system. Contax later launched the autofocus N series, but C/Y users stayed loyal to the RTS III. When Kyocera left the camera business in 2005, the RTS III became the final chapter of Contax's manual-focus flagships.

The RTS III is still highly sought after on the second-hand market today. Many owners consider it the peak of what a 35mm manual-focus SLR could achieve.

Conclusion

The RTS III is a camera that refused to follow fashion. When autofocus became the norm, it went back to photography's most basic question: how to make images sharper and more accurate. The ceramic vacuum pressure plate, the 1/8000 sec shutter and the full-coverage viewfinder all serve image quality. For photographers who appreciate Zeiss lenses, the RTS III is more than a camera. It is the best stage there is for Zeiss optics.
