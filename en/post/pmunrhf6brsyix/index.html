Contax RTS: The Legendary SLR That United Germany and Japan

Contax RTS: The Legendary SLR That United Germany and Japan

The Contax RTS holds a special place in the history of the single-lens reflex. German optics, Japanese manufacturing and Porsche design came together in one camera. It appeared in the mid-1970s and began a new chapter for the Contax name that lasted three decades.

The revival of the Contax name

Contax began in the 1930s as a Zeiss Ikon rangefinder brand and was once a serious rival to Leica. After the war, Zeiss Ikon's camera business shrank steadily. In the early 1970s it stopped making cameras altogether, and the Contax name fell silent.

Although Zeiss had left camera manufacturing, it still wanted a high-end body for its lenses. At the same time, Yashica in Japan needed a prestigious brand to lift its image. The fit was obvious. Zeiss licensed the Contax name to Yashica. Yashica would build the bodies and Zeiss would design the lenses. The first product of that partnership was the Contax RTS.

A body shaped by Porsche Design

The Porsche Design team took part in shaping the RTS. The body has clean, crisp lines that are angular without being harsh, the control dials are logically arranged, and it sits comfortably in the hand. Most Japanese SLRs of the day piled on features with little thought for form, so the RTS looked strikingly modern beside them. Even by today's standards it doesn't look dated.

What "Real Time System" meant

RTS stands for "Real Time System". The camera's headline feature was its electromagnetic shutter release. A conventional mechanical release has to travel a fair distance before it fires the shutter. The RTS release has a very short travel, and the lightest touch trips it. This greatly reduces the delay between pressing the button and the moment of exposure. For photographers shooting moving subjects and chasing the decisive moment, that was a genuine advantage.

The same "real time" idea ran through the whole system. The camera could take a power winder and a motor drive, so its responsiveness and shooting speed could keep up with professional needs.

An electronic body

The RTS belonged to the new generation of electronic SLRs that arrived in the mid-1970s. Its shutter was electronically controlled. It offered aperture-priority automatic exposure and full manual mode, and indicator lights in the viewfinder showed the shutter speed. This let photographers keep track of exposure while composing. The body relied on a battery, which was still a relatively new approach at the time and reflected Yashica's experience with electronic cameras.

The Zeiss T* lens range

For many buyers, the Zeiss lenses were the main reason to choose Contax. The RTS uses the Contax/Yashica mount, usually shortened to C/Y. It was served by a range of Zeiss lenses with T* multicoating, including classic Planar, Distagon and Sonnar designs. The Planar 50mm f/1.4 and Planar 85mm f/1.4 are the best loved. They are known for rich colour, a strong sense of depth and smooth out-of-focus rendering, which many people call the "Zeiss look".

Some of these lenses were made in Germany, and production later moved to Japan, but Zeiss kept control of the optical design. C/Y lenses are still in high demand on the second-hand market, and many photographers adapt them to mirrorless bodies to give them a second life.

The RTS family

After the success of the RTS, Contax brought out successors. The RTS II arrived in the early 1980s with a quartz-controlled shutter for greater accuracy. The RTS III followed in the 1990s. Its innovations included a ceramic vacuum pressure plate that kept the film perfectly flat, and it took the RTS line to its peak. Kyocera later acquired Yashica, and the Contax range continued under Kyocera until the company left the camera business in 2005, which brought the Contax name to a close.

Conclusion

The Contax RTS matters for more than being an excellent camera. It proved that German optics and Japanese manufacturing could be combined successfully. Zeiss lenses, Yashica's build quality and Porsche's design sense together created a system that lasted thirty years. Pick up an RTS today and you can still feel that hair-trigger shutter release and see the distinctive images the Zeiss lenses produce. That is why the camera is so fondly remembered.
