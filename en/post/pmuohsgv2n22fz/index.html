Classic Zeiss Optical Designs: The Code Behind the Lens Names

Classic Zeiss Optical Designs: The Code Behind the Lens Names

Pick up a Zeiss lens and you will always find a name engraved on the barrel: Planar, Sonnar, Distagon and so on. These are not arbitrary model numbers. Each one stands for a particular optical formula with its own history. Once you understand these designs, you can see why different Zeiss lenses produce images with such different characters.

Protar: where the anastigmat began

By the late nineteenth century, Zeiss was already the authority on microscope optics. Led by the scientist Paul Rudolph, it then moved into photographic lenses. Rudolph's Protar appeared in 1890. It was one of the earliest anastigmats, lenses that improved sharpness in the centre and at the edges at the same time. Few people mention the Protar today, but it laid the foundations of Zeiss photographic optics, and several classic designs grew out of it.

Planar: the model of symmetry

In 1896 Rudolph designed the Planar. It is based on the Double Gauss formula. The front and rear groups are broadly symmetrical, which keeps aberrations well under control and gives a flat, even image. That is where the name comes from.

The Planar has many glass-to-air surfaces, though, and before lens coatings existed it was prone to flare, so it did not catch on at first. Only once coating technology matured did the Planar reach its potential. It is now the most common formula for fast standard lenses. The Contax Planar 50mm f/1.4 and 85mm f/1.4, and the Hasselblad Planar 80mm f/2.8, are among the great lenses of photographic history.

The most legendary Planar is a 50mm with an aperture of f/0.7. It was originally made for the space programme and was later used by the director Stanley Kubrick to shoot scenes lit only by candlelight in Barry Lyndon, which has become a famous story in film history.

Tessar: the simple "eagle eye"

In 1902 Rudolph introduced the Tessar. With just four elements in three groups it is simple, yet it delivers excellent sharpness and contrast, and it earned the nickname "eagle eye" (Adlerauge).

The Tessar is compact, cheap and easy to make. That made it one of the most widely produced lens designs of the twentieth century, found in everything from folding cameras and rangefinders to pancake lenses. Many manufacturers drew on the Tessar patent, and its influence spread through the whole camera industry.

Sonnar: a pioneer of fast apertures

The optical designer Ludwig Bertele created the Sonnar around 1930. Its defining feature is cementing several elements together to reduce the number of glass-to-air surfaces. Before coatings matured, this cut flare effectively and made possible apertures that were rare at the time.

The Sonnar made its name on Zeiss Ikon's Contax rangefinders, and the 50mm f/1.5 was among the fastest standard lenses of its day. Sonnars give high contrast with soft out-of-focus areas, and the formula later became the main design for short and medium telephotos, such as the Contax Sonnar 135mm f/2.8 and the Hasselblad Sonnar 150mm f/4.

Biogon: the height of rangefinder wide-angles

The Biogon was also Bertele's work. The post-war Biogon uses an almost symmetrical ultra-wide formula, with the rear elements sitting very close to the film. This gives extremely low distortion and excellent edge quality.

The design needs the back of the lens to reach deep into the body, though, so it only suits rangefinder cameras without a mirror. The classic example is the Biogon 38mm f/4.5 on the Hasselblad SWC, which is still regarded as one of the best wide-angle lenses ever made for controlling distortion.

Distagon: a wide-angle built for the SLR

An SLR's mirror needs room to move, so the back of the lens can't sit too close to the film, and a Biogon-style design won't fit. Zeiss's answer was the Distagon. It uses a retrofocus formula: a large negative group at the front extends the back focus and leaves space for the mirror.

The Distagon became Zeiss's standard design for SLR wide-angles. The Contax Distagon 35mm f/1.4 and 28mm f/2, and the Hasselblad Distagon 50mm, are all highly regarded.

Hologon: a minimalist ultra-wide experiment

The Hologon is an unusual design. It achieves an ultra-wide angle of view with very few elements. Its aperture is small and fixed, but distortion is superbly controlled. Few were made, and it is now a collector's piece, an optical experiment that shows how bold Zeiss could be.

Conclusion

From Protar to Hologon, each Zeiss lens name stands for a way of solving an optical problem. The Planar aims for flatness and the Tessar for simplicity. The Sonnar pushed apertures wider, while the Biogon and Distagon solved the wide-angle problem for rangefinders and SLRs respectively. Once you know these designs, a Zeiss lens is more than a name. It carries more than a century of optical thinking.
