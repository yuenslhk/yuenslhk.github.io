New Voigtländer: A Second Life for an Old Name

New Voigtländer: A Second Life for an Old Name

Few camera brands have a longer history than Voigtländer. The brand photographers now call "New Voigtländer" is the story of an old German name and a Japanese optical factory in Nagano.

A Viennese optical dynasty

Johann Christoph Voigtländer founded the company in Vienna in 1756, first making optical and surveying instruments. In 1840, Voigtländer began producing a portrait lens designed by the mathematician Joseph Petzval. With a maximum aperture of around f/3.6, it was far faster than the lenses of its day. It cut daguerreotype exposure times from minutes to seconds, and that made portrait photography practical. The firm later moved to Braunschweig in Germany, and in the twentieth century it made cameras such as the Bessa, Vito and Prominent, becoming an important part of the German camera industry.

After the war, the rise of Japanese cameras made life increasingly hard for German makers. Voigtländer first came under the Zeiss group, and in the early 1970s the brand passed to Rollei. The trademark then changed hands several times, and a name with two centuries of history was reduced to an empty shell.

Cosina steps in: the turning point of 1999

In 1999, Cosina, based in Nakano in Japan's Nagano Prefecture, gained the right to use the Voigtländer name. Founded in 1959, Cosina had spent decades as a contract manufacturer. Many of the entry-level SLRs people remember owning actually came off its production lines. Its president, Hirofumi Kobayashi, is a passionate camera enthusiast. He spotted a market nobody else was paying attention to. Even as autofocus was taking over, some photographers still wanted manual, mechanical and finely made cameras and lenses.

That is how "New Voigtländer" was born. Cosina did not simply put an old badge on new products. It revived names from Voigtländer's past to define a new product line.

The Bessa series: the affordable rangefinder returns

New Voigtländer's first product was the Bessa-L, a rangefinder-style camera with an L39 screw mount. It launched alongside the 15mm f/4.5 Super Wide-Heliar. At the time, a 15mm lens for a rangefinder was both rare and expensive, so this compact and reasonably priced ultra-wide caused a stir.

The Bessa-R that followed added a coupled rangefinder. The R2, R3 and R4 series moved to the M mount and offered different viewfinder magnifications for everything from wide-angle to short-telephoto work. For anyone who wanted to try rangefinder photography but couldn't afford a Leica, the Bessa was the most practical way in. Cosina went on to make the Zeiss Ikon rangefinder and the ZM lenses for Zeiss, which shows the standard of its manufacturing. The Bessa line was discontinued around 2015, but it still has a loyal following on the second-hand market.

Lens names: a code carried over from history

Voigtländer is best known today for its lenses. Cosina kept the historic Voigtländer lens names and broadly assigns them by aperture:

  • Nokton: the fast series, usually f/1.5 or faster. Examples include the 35mm f/1.2, the 50mm f/1.1 and even a 50mm f/1.0.
  • Ultron: moderate apertures, mostly between f/1.7 and f/2.
  • Color-Skopar: slower, lightweight lenses known for their small size.
  • Heliar: used mainly for ultra-wides and special designs.
  • APO-Lanthar: high-specification apochromatic designs and the brand's flagship range in recent years. The 50mm f/2 APO-Lanthar, for example, has been widely praised for its exceptional resolution.

Most of these lenses have all-metal barrels and manual focus. The focus rings are smoothly damped and the aperture rings click crisply, and few rivals at the same price match the build quality.

A new stage in the mirrorless era

The rise of digital mirrorless cameras opened up an even bigger market for New Voigtländer. Their short flange distances let rangefinder lenses be used through adapters. Cosina took the opportunity to release native lenses for Sony E, Nikon Z, Fujifilm X and Micro Four Thirds. Many of them have electronic contacts, which pass Exif data to the camera and work with in-body stabilisation and focus magnification. They keep the pleasure of manual focus while fitting into a modern workflow. The f/0.95 Nokton lenses for Micro Four Thirds have also become favourites with many filmmakers.

Conclusion

New Voigtländer succeeded because it knew exactly where it stood. It never tried to beat the big makers on autofocus or speed. Instead, it concentrated on manual focus, mechanical feel and optical character. It brought an all-but-vanished German name back to life, and it let a generation of photographers enjoy slow focusing and careful composition at a reasonable price. More than two and a half centuries on, the Voigtländer name still appears on the front of lenses, and that alone makes it a story worth telling.
